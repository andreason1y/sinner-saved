That Saturday night I came home from youth worship. Our group's discussion topic that night was John 15 — the vine and the branches. A simple part, maybe I have even heard it many times since childhood. But for some reason, that night the image wouldn't leave my mind. I lay down, but couldn't sleep. The questions kept coming, and one by one they opened up light that had eluded me.

Perhaps you have felt the same fatigue as I have felt all this time: tired of being a Christian. Not tired of persecution or great sacrifice — but tired of constantly trying. Try to be good. Try to be patient. Try to serve sincerely. Try to control yourself. It's as if spiritual life is a long list that must be fulfilled with our own strength, and we always feel lacking.

Revisiting the Image of Jesus

That night I realized, perhaps there is one image from Jesus' own mouth that we need to look at again. On the last night before being crucified, in the midst of parting with His disciples, Jesus did not give them a ministry strategy or a list of tasks. He gives a simple picture of a garden: a vine and branches. And within that picture is hidden a secret that, if we understand, can change the way we live our entire life of faith. Let me share what I discovered that night.

Why the Vine?

“I am the true vine, and my Father is the producer,” Jesus said in John 15:1. At first glance, this is just a beautiful metaphor. But why wine? Among so many trees — olives, figs, majestic cedars — why was the vine chosen?

The answer stretches far back, throughout the Old Testament. Israel is repeatedly depicted as a vine. Psalm 80 refers to the people as grapes brought out of Egypt and then planted by God. Isaiah 5 sings the melancholy “Song of the Vineyard”: God planted choice grapes, tended them lovingly, but all they produced were sour fruit. Jeremiah, Hosea, and Ezekiel repeated the same tune — Israel was the vine that failed to bear the fruit it should.

So when Jesus said, “I am the true vine,” He was speaking something much deeper than a simple parable. He claimed Himself to be the true Israel — finally bearing righteous fruit before the Father, something the chosen people had failed to achieve for centuries. The word “righteous” is not a decoration; it is a declaration.

What Exactly Is “Fruit”?

We often hear that Christians “must bear fruit.” But what exactly is the fruit that Jesus was referring to? Interestingly, He never defines it directly in John 15. So we have to read it from context — and the context speaks quite clearly.

Right after speaking about fruitfulness, Jesus turns to one recurring theme: love. “Abide in My love,” He said (verse 9). "This is My commandment, that you love one another" (verse 12, then repeated in verse 17). The Greek word used for “dwelling” here is μένω (menō) — not a temporary visit, but a permanent residence. If the fruit is something else, why is the explanation that follows about love? The most natural reading is: the main fruit that Jesus refers to is real love — the love that flows among His disciples.

This is in harmony with what Paul calls “the fruit of the Spirit” in Galatians 5: love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control. Notice that Paul calls it καρπός (karpos, “fruit”) in the singular, not the plural. This is not a list of separate accomplishments that we must collect one by one, but rather a unity of character — a living manifestation of Christ growing out from within us.

A Subtle Mistake: Trying to Become Our Own Tree

Now we come to the point that is often missed. So far, many of us have lived our spiritual lives with an assumption that we have never expressed: that we are the source of our own goodness. We try to be moral, try to be harmonious, try to serve — all with our own energy, as if a branch could produce fruit without a tree.

Jesus closes this gap with a sentence that barely leaves room: “Apart from Me you can do nothing” (v. 5). Not “you can, but it would be better with Me.” But can do nothing — zero. All morality, service, and self-mastery that is powered by one's own strength produces branches that are busy, but disconnected from life.

Then Effort is Not Erased — Only Diverted

Does this mean we stop trying altogether? No. “Abiding” is not passivity, it is not sitting around waiting for the fruit to appear by itself. Just stay active. Jesus himself explains it: letting His word abide in us (verse 7), praying, obeying His commandments, and remaining in His love (verses 9–10).

The shift is not from trying to relaxing, but from effort that replaces relationships to effort that flows from relationships. This is a reversal that is both relieving and striking: we remain disciplined, we continue to fight — but what we are fighting for is no longer the result, but rather closeness to the source. Same energy, different direction. Not “I must produce,” but “I absorb, and then life flows.”

Why Being Fruitful Means Being Pruned

There is a part of this picture that is uncomfortable, but it is too important to miss. "Every branch in Me that does not bear fruit, He cuts off, and every branch that does bear fruit, He cleanses, so that it may bear more fruit" (verse 2).

Pay attention to the sharp point: what is cleaned — pruned — is precisely the branch that is already bearing fruit. Pruning is not a sign of failure. Often it is actually a sign that your life is being productive, and the Father wants more. The pruning knife applies to living branches, not dead ones.

Pruning does hurt. He cut off shoots that looked healthy, energy that seemed wasted. Plants seem to be missing something good. Hebrews 12:11 admits it honestly: discipline “does not bring joy in its season, but sorrow” — only then does it bear the fruit of righteousness. And what is often pruned is not overt sin, but rather the good things that secretly sap the energy from our best fruit: busyness, attachment, legitimate but distracting ambitions.

Conclusion: Back to Basics

In the end, John 15 is not a command to try harder. It is a call to return — back to the One from whom all our lives flow. Maybe what needs to change in us is not "knowing more," but rather shifting from being busy managing our spiritual life to being willing to accept life. It's simpler, and actually more difficult for those who are used to feeling capable.

And if you realize that all this time you have been trying to become your own tree, don't despair. A branch that realizes its error does not need to reconnect itself from scratch — it remains anchored to the tree; the sap is still flowing. As soon as we stop and look back at Him, life comes back to us. Even this awareness itself is a fruit — for the branch never makes itself aware; It is the tree that awakens him.

I am the vine and you are the branches. Whoever abides in Me and I in him, he bears much fruit, for apart from Me you can do nothing.

John 15:5
