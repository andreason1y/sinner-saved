There is a conversation that I heard more than once. Someone said, "It's a personal choice, as long as it doesn't hurt anyone else." And that sentence seems reasonable, until you start to ask, is it true that no one gets hurt?

We live in an age that separates sex from everything that ever gave it meaning. From commitment. Of loyalty. From questions about who we are and what we were created for. All that remains is approval and satisfaction. And the Bible, quietly but firmly, rejects that framework.

Not because the Bible is ancient or irrelevant. But because the Bible starts from a different place: not from "to what extent humans are allowed," but from "what humans were created for."

Our bodies have "memory"

Before getting into the Bible text, there is something interesting from the world of science that is worth our attention.

Neuroscientific research on pair bonding, the intimate attachment between two individuals, shows that sexual relationships are never just physical. There are active neural circuits. There is oxytocin which encourages trust and closeness. There is dopamine that creates a strong association with your partner. There is vasopressin involved in long-term relationship stability.

In short: the human body is designed to bond, not just interact. Intimacy leaves traces — not just in memories, but biologically.

A study published in Scientific Reports (2025) even found that a person's sexual relationship history is related to certain future relational outcomes. It's not about judging — it's about recognizing that our bodies are not machines that can be used without profound consequences.

And here's where I start to see something: it turns out the Bible was talking about this long before there were neuroscience laboratories.

"The two became one flesh"

Genesis 2:24 is one of the most frequently quoted verses about marriage, but I think we rarely really stop to feel it. "Therefore a man will leave his father and mother and be united to his wife, and the two will become one flesh."

Notice the pattern: leaving, joining, becoming one. This is not an administrative rule. This is a description of what actually happens when two people enter into a sexual relationship — something is united.

Jesus quotes the same verse in Matthew 19 and adds something important: "What God has joined together, let no man separate." That union is not just an agreement between two people — there is God involved there.

But it is Paul who makes this implication really poignant. In 1 Corinthians 6:16, he wrote that whoever commits himself to a sexually immoral woman remains "in one body" with her. This means that unity does not only occur in a legal marriage. Sexual relationships — wherever they occur — create a real form of attachment.

This changes things. Sex is not a neutral act. Whenever two people come together sexually, there is something happening on a deeper level than we realize. Neuroscientists call it pair bonding. The Bible calls it one flesh. Both point in the same direction.

Why it's not about rules, but about corruption

The prohibition against adultery in the Bible is often read as a list of moral prohibitions. But I think there's a deeper way to read it: it's a protection of the human capacity to love fully.

If our bodies are designed for bonding, and every sexual encounter leaves a visible trace, then reckless sex doesn't just break the rules — it breaks something. It trains us to connect then separate, connect then separate, until the capacity to truly bond becomes more difficult.

Hebrews 13:4 says, "Let all of you be respectful of marriage and do not defile the bed." 1 Thessalonians 4:3-5 calls sexual holiness the will of God — not a spiritual addition, but the essence of what it means to know God.

But there is one moment in the Gospel that always gets me. stop. When a woman caught in adultery was brought before Jesus — not to be exhorted, but to be punished — He did not react as anyone expected. He actually said in John 8:11: "Neither do I condemn you. Go, and sin no more."

Grace and truth, in one short sentence. Jesus did not consider sin light. But He also doesn't close the door.

This is not about shame

I want to speak directly to anyone who is reading this and feels that their sexual journey has gone too far, is too messy to repair.

1 John 1:9 is a very concrete promise: "If we confess our sins, He is faithful and just, so He will forgive us all our sins and cleanse us from all unrighteousness." Not some sins. Not just small sins. All sin.

And 1 Corinthians 6:19-20 — a verse often used to claim holiness — actually begins with the fact of redemption: "Your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit... you have been bought and the price has been paid in full." The call to holiness is not a threat. This is an invitation — because your body has been redeemed, you can live differently.

Holiness without grace breeds despair. Grace without holiness turns into justification for sin. What the Gospel offers is both at once: truly forgiven, and truly changed.

Then what do we want?

Romans 12:2 says we must "be transformed by the renewing of your mind." That starts with stopping calling sin by a gentler name. Sex outside of marriage, pornography, unholy relationships — these need to be acknowledged for what they are, not wrapped up in more convenient language.

But repentance is not a solitary project. Many people fail not because they don't know what is right, but because they walk alone. The church needs to be a place where these conversations can happen honestly — not just when there's a scandal, but as a normal part of living together.

And for anyone who feels like their past is too much to bear: the Christian faith does not recognize the concept of being “broken forever.” In Christ, the sinful past is not the last word. Forgiveness is available. Recovery is possible. And the first step can be started today.
