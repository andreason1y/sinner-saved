There is one question that keeps coming up: what are humans actually made of? Not bones and flesh, that's clear. But our inner side: soul, feelings, will, and that “something” deep down that longs for God. Are they one and the same, or two different things? This question sounds abstract, but the answer touches the way we pray, grow, and know ourselves before God.

Throughout history, Christians have converged on two answers. The first is called dichotomy: humans consist of two parts, body and “mind”, where soul and spirit are considered simply two names for the same thing. The second is called trichotomy: a human being consists of three parts, body, soul, and spirit, which can be distinguished. Here I try to weigh both honestly: what does the Bible say, and why did the church ultimately choose the path it did?

Three Parts: Body, Soul, and Spirit

Try to imagine yourself like a three-story house. The body is the ground floor, where you greet the world through the senses. The soul is the middle floor, the space where your thoughts, feelings, and will reside; this is where the “me” you recognize every day lives. And the spirit is the top floor, the most private room, where humans meet and worship God directly.

According to the trichotomy view, it is precisely this spirit that makes humans different from all other creatures. Animals have bodies and have “souls” in the sense of consciousness and instincts, but only humans have a special organ to contact their Creator. Dichotomy does not deny this depth; it only holds that “soul” and “spirit” are two viewpoints on the inner life that is one and the same.

What Does the Original Language Say?

The Bible was not written in Indonesian, so we look at the original words. In the Hebrew Old Testament, there are two words: nephesh (soul) and ruach (spirit). Interestingly, both are not used carelessly. Nephesh appears hundreds of times and is almost always tied to flesh-and-blood life; Leviticus 17:11 even says that life (nephesh) is in the blood. Ruach, meanwhile, is more often used for divine breath, spiritual power, and things related to God.

Zechariah 12:1 speaks of GOD “who formed man's spirit within him.” Note: not just “gave breath”, but formed something special within man. This sounds like more than just another word for “life.”

Of course, there are also places where these two words seem to be used interchangeably. When Rachel was dying in Genesis 35:18, what was mentioned was nephesh (soul); in other texts, the word ruach (spirit) is used for similar death scenes. This is an honest fact and should not be hidden. But note: words that are sometimes swapped do not automatically mean exactly the same thing, a point we will come back to.

In the Greek New Testament, the pattern continues: psyche for soul, pneuma for spirit. The Apostle Paul, in 1 Corinthians 2, sharply differentiates “soul men” (who live only from their own reason and feelings) from “spiritual men” (who live led by the Spirit). These are not two names for the same person; these are two different spiritual states, one still alive of itself, one already quickened by the Spirit of God. If soul and spirit were truly identical, this sharp distinction would lose its meaning.

“My soul magnifies the Lord, and my spirit rejoices in God my Savior.”

Luke 1:46-47

See Mary's song above. She uses two different words in the same breath: soul and spirit. A poet can repeat an idea with different words for beauty. But she can also deliberately name two things because she feels two different dimensions in herself responding to God. The second possibility is equally strong, and should not be dismissed out of hand.

Two Verses That Are Often Key

Two verses are most often used to support the three-part view. The first, 1 Thessalonians 5:23: “May God … sanctify you completely, your spirit, soul, and body.” In the original language, Paul uses the same word “and” as when we separate three different things. He does not pile up two synonyms; he mentions three things.

Secondly, Hebrews 4:12, which states that God's word is able to “separate soul and spirit.” In the same verse, the author uses the image “joints and marrow.” Joints and marrow are clearly two different things, very close, difficult to separate, but clearly different. If the companion picture is like that, it makes sense that soul and spirit are likewise: different, very close, and only the sword of God's word can penetrate between them.

There is a simple picture that can help. Imagine a teabag in a cup of hot water. The water and tea are now integrated, difficult to separate, and they seep into each other to the bottom of the cup. But no one would say that water and tea leaves are the same thing. That's how soul and spirit are: so closely integrated in our daily life that they often feel like one, but are still two things with different origins and functions. What unites the two does not mean merging them into identity.

Then What About the Opposite Argument?

Supporters of two parts have objections that should be heard, not ignored. Let's consider the ones that come up most often.

“Why does Mark 12:30 only mention the heart, soul, mind, and strength, without the spirit?”

The answer: here Jesus is quoting Deuteronomy 6:5 to express the totality of love, not compiling a list of human parts. The proof is that the three Gospels record different combinations. If this were an official list of human structure, it should be uniform. The absence of the word “spirit” here does not prove that the spirit does not exist; it only shows that Jesus was talking about love, not about the anatomy of the soul.

“Don't many verses only mention two parts, like ‘body and soul’?”

That's right. But this is the principle of healthy Bible reading: a verse that mentions two things in one context does not invalidate another verse that mentions three things in a different context. The Bible writer was not obliged to mention the entire human structure every time he said “man.” Ecclesiastes 12:7 speaks about death in general; it's natural to be concise.

Being silent about something does not mean denying it. Context determines what needs to be said, not what is.

“But John 4:24 mentions ‘worship in the spirit’, and that is ambiguous.”

It is true, and this is the strongest objection. There Jesus was answering the question of where to worship (on which mountain?), not the question of human inner structure. The “spirit” in that verse could refer to the Holy Spirit, the human spirit, or both. But herein lies the point: an ambiguous verse cannot be used to prove anything with certainty, including it cannot be used to prove a dichotomy.

The Crux of the Problem

If summarized, the difference between the two views boils down to one thing. Proponents of two parts often point to verses where soul and spirit seem to be used interchangeably, then conclude: they mean the same thing. But this logic has a loophole. Two words that are sometimes interchanged do not automatically mean identical.

Just think about the words “mind” and “heart” in our conversations. Sometimes we say “follow your heart” even though we mean reasoning; sometimes the opposite. The two may stand in for each other in casual conversation, but no one really considers the heart and the brain to be the same organ. So are soul and spirit: flexible in use, but still distinguishable.

So a wiser principle is this: let the verses that clearly distinguish soul and spirit be the main guide, then understand the concise verses in the light of the clear verses, not the other way around. This approach is what makes the trichotomy able to explain all the data: it can explain when soul and spirit are used interchangeably, as well as when the two are intentionally differentiated.

Why Did the Church Finally Choose Two Parts?

If the data tends to support three parts, why do the majority of churches actually adhere to two parts? The answer lies in history. In the first centuries, many church fathers, especially among the Greek speakers, actually tended to be trichotomous. Figures such as Justin Martyr and Gregory of Nyssa read humanity as three parts.

The turning point occurred in the fourth century. A figure named Apolinarius used the three-part concept to construct a false teaching about Christ: he said Jesus had a human body and soul, but not a human spirit. The church council of 381 rejected this teaching. Unfortunately, in the process of rejecting Apollinarius, the three-part concept was swept aside from the mainstream.

After that, Augustine, one of the most influential thinkers in church history, strengthened the two-part view, in part because he came from a philosophy that viewed the soul as an indivisible whole. Its influence was so great that over the centuries, two parts became the official position of the Western church, then inherited by the Reformed tradition to this day.

There is a subtle irony here. The Reformed tradition strongly upholds the principle of “the Bible alone” (sola scriptura). But in this matter, their position rests more on Augustine's legacy than on analysis of the text itself. This is not an accusation; just an honest account of how a view can persist because of the weight of tradition, not simply because of textual evidence.

Here's what explains the gap you might feel: on the one hand, the words of the Bible tend to distinguish between soul and spirit; on the other hand, the majority of official churches teach two parts. The reason is not because the text changed, but because after the fourth century, discussions about humans no longer started from the text afresh, but from a framework that had already been established. The three-part view has not disappeared; it lives on, especially in circles that emphasize spiritual life and the experience of communion with God rather than systematic doctrinal formation.

A Balance That Needs to Be Maintained

Before we get too excited, there is one important signpost. The three-part view can also be misused. If we draw too sharp a line between soul and spirit, as if they were two separate boxes operating independently, we are mistaken. The Bible always views humans as one complete person. Body, soul, and spirit interpenetrate; they are not three occupants living in each other's locked rooms.

So the distinction between soul and spirit is best understood as a difference in function, different roles within one person, not a separation into three different “things.” This is the balance that keeps us from two abysses: melting everything into one, or breaking people into pieces.

Why This Is Important for Your Life

Maybe you ask: what's in it for my daily faith? First, let it be clear: this debate does not determine salvation. People who adhere to two parts and three parts can equally be true Christians. This is not a test of orthodoxy.

To sum up, there are four things we can carry from this discussion. First, the original biblical words, nephesh and ruach, psyche and pneuma, show different usage patterns, not just two names for one thing. Second, verses such as 1 Thessalonians 5:23 and Hebrews 4:12 openly mention the three elements and distinguish the soul from the spirit. Third, objections to the three-part view generally rely on concise or ambiguous verses, and such verses are not strong enough to invalidate clear verses. Fourth, the dominance of the two-part view is more the fruit of history than the result of pure exegesis.

But a more precise understanding remains fruitful. When you realize there is a “deepest space” within yourself, the spirit, specifically designed for communion with God, prayer changes from an exercise of thoughts and feelings (soul) to an encounter on a deeper level. Spiritual growth also feels clearer: not just improving behavior or managing emotions, but allowing the spirit enlivened by the Holy Spirit to lead the whole being. This is the essence of the worship “in spirit and truth” that Jesus intended.

Knowing Ourselves, Knowing Him

When we read the Bible honestly, paying attention to the original words, the context, and the entire verse, the three-part view stands on a more solid foundation. The distinction between soul and spirit is not just a figure of speech, but a consistent pattern from Genesis to Paul's letters.

The two-part view is neither wrong nor heretical; it is simply a legacy of the way the Western church reads the Bible through a particular lens. But for those of you who want to know yourself as God designed you, recognizing the spirit as a space of encounter with Him can be a door to a deeper life of prayer and growth. Because in the end, knowing who we are always comes down to one thing: knowing Him who made us.
